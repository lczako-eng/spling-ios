The speaker in traffic is the problem.

A human-run drive-through gets the order right about 85 times in 100. The AI voice systems replacing them currently do a little worse. The reasons aren’t mysterious: road noise, cheap speakers, accents, second languages, speech differences. And if the spoken channel fails you entirely — because you’re deaf, because you use an AAC device, because today your voice won’t cooperate — the error rate isn’t 15%. It’s exclusion from a transaction everyone else finishes in ninety seconds.

Everyone else is trying to make the speaker hear better. Spling deletes the speaker. Your order is composed as data and arrives as data. Remove the acoustic channel, and every failure mode attached to it goes too. You order. They understand. Every time.

What travels with you

  • Your language

    Order in whatever language you think in. The restaurant’s register receives exact items from its own menu, in its own language — never a guess, never free text.

  • Your profile

    How you communicate, what you’re allergic to, what “the usual” means — said once, applied everywhere. An anaphylaxis allergen doesn’t rely on you remembering to mention it; Spling blocks the item before the order exists.

  • Your record

    Spling keeps both halves of every order: what you asked for, and what actually arrived. Over time that becomes an accuracy record per restaurant — the receipt finally has a memory.

Ordering for someone you love

Sometimes the person ordering isn’t the person eating. A parent, a support worker, a friend can stage the whole order — right items, right modifiers, allergens already enforced — and the person it belongs to finishes it with a single tap. Their order, their confirmation, their independence. Just less typing.
