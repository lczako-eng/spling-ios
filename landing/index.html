02 — The problem

The speaker in traffic is the problem.

Everyone else is trying to make the speaker hear better. On screen, a noisy audio waveform flattens into a straight line, striking through the words “the speaker”. The heading “Spling deletes the speaker.” appears, and the flat line becomes the dashed rule of a small printed order ticket reading one large latte, oat milk — sound becoming structured data. A human-run drive-through gets the order right about 85 times in 100.1 The AI voice systems replacing them currently do a little worse. The reasons aren’t mysterious: road noise, cheap speakers, accents, second languages, speech differences. And if the spoken channel fails you entirely — because you’re deaf, because you use an AAC device, because today your voice won’t cooperate — the error rate isn’t 15%. It’s exclusion from a transaction everyone else finishes in ninety seconds.

Remove the acoustic channel, and every failure mode attached to it goes too. You order. They understand. Every time.

The drive-through is only the clearest version of this. The same barrier stands at a café counter, a hotel desk, an airport gate, a pharmacy window, a stadium concession and a government service desk — anywhere a person has to make themselves understood to a business before anything can happen. That barrier is the problem. The speaker was just where we noticed it.

04 — Everywhere commerce happens

The counter is everywhere.

Every one of these is the same transaction wearing different clothes: a person has something specific in mind, and a business needs it exactly. The only thing standing between them is communication.

  • Drive-throughsNoise, a low-fidelity speaker, and a queue behind you.
  • Counters & cafésA busy room, an accent either way, and a line that makes repeating yourself expensive.
  • HotelsRequests that matter — accessibility, allergies, a late arrival — made in a second language at midnight.
  • AirportsYou are, by definition, somewhere your language may not be spoken.
  • RetailSpecifics: the size, the variant, the exact thing rather than the nearest thing.
  • Hospitals & pharmaciesWhere being misunderstood carries the highest cost of anywhere on this list.
  • Stadiums & venuesLoud by design, timed by an interval, and impossible to negotiate through.
  • Government service desksWhere the person least able to be heard is often the one who most needs to be.

Spling is not a food-ordering app that might expand. It is a communication layer, and ordering is simply the first thing we pointed it at — because it is the highest-volume, most-measurable version of the same problem.

05 — Context

You arrive. It already knows.

The hardest part of being understood is everything you have to establish first.

  • Where you are, and which business you’re at — so you are not searching for a merchant while standing in front of them.
  • What they actually offer right now — the live menu or services, not last season’s.
  • Your language — already set, never asked for again.
  • Your communication profile — already applied, never re-explained.
  • Your dietary and accessibility requirements — enforced before anything can go wrong, not recited by you under time pressure.
  • Your preferences — what you order, what you send back, what you never want again.

Location is only the trigger. Anyone can draw a circle on a map — the circle is not the innovation. The context that loads inside it is. The measure of this working is that nothing has to be established twice, ever again.

06 — Why it comes out right

Every hop loses something.

An order isn’t one event, it’s a relay. Each hand-off is a chance to lose a word — and the analogue ones lose the most, because sound is the least reliable thing in the chain. Fewer hops, clearer order. That’s the whole engineering thesis.

Seven chances to drop a word. You find out at the window.

Composed with Spling 2 hops

  1. You meancheeseburger, no onion
  2. Composed as dataITEM_2210 · MOD_NO_ONION · QTY 1
  3. Rendered at the till1× Cheeseburger · No Onion

No air, no microphone, no speaker, no recall. Nothing to mishear.

The middle isn’t a language. That’s the point.

Spling doesn’t translate your words into the cashier’s words — translation is just another lossy hop. It converts what you meant into item IDs, modifier IDs and quantities: a small, exact, wordless format that belongs to no language at all.

Every language on earth can enter it, and it comes out the other side as whatever that till speaks. Hungarian in, English out. Thai in, French out. Nothing is “nearly right”, because the middle has no room for nearly — an item either exists on that menu or the order doesn’t leave. A universal language for one narrow thing, which is exactly why it works.

07 — Who this is for

Built for the people communication fails.

It is one failure wearing different faces. Language leaves a person and doesn’t arrive — because of a speaker, an accent, a stammer, a second language, or a language that was never made of sound. The reason changes every time. The failure doesn’t, and neither does the fix: stop sending language through the air. That isn’t a setting you switch on. It’s the reason the product exists.

  • If the local language isn’t yours — you order in the one you think in, and the kitchen reads its own menu back. Everything below is this same problem, arriving by a different route.
  • If you have a speech difference — a stammer, a lisp, dysarthria, apraxia, a voice that cooperates on some days and not others — you are not asking anyone to be patient. You are not repeating yourself into a box while a queue builds behind you. And if your assistant mishears the same words the same way every time, Spling learns that once, quietly, and stops it happening again.
  • If you’re deaf or hard of hearing — half of this transaction was always the reply, and the reply is the part nobody builds for. “Was that a medium?” through a blown speaker, in a car, with an engine running, is not a question you can answer. Here there is no crackling reply to miss, nothing to confirm by ear, and no moment where you nod at something you didn’t catch and hope. The answer arrives in writing, and it stays there.
  • If you served — noise damage is one of the most common lasting injuries of service, and a drive-through speaker is precisely where it shows up. This isn’t an accommodation you’re asking for. It’s the order being right.
  • If reading the board is the problem — dyslexia, low vision, a language you speak but don’t read, or a board that changed since last time. Nobody reads a menu well at speed with a queue behind them. Your assistant reads it; you answer in words.
  • If you sign — ASL and LSQ are languages, not workarounds for not having one, and neither of them is English. Being handed a notepad at a counter means writing your second language at speed with a queue behind you. Spling never asks for it: the language happens with your assistant, and the counter only ever receives the finished order.
  • If you use AAC — your device already composes language. Spling takes it from there. Nothing has to be spoken aloud to a stranger through a speaker.
  • If you’re non-verbal — this transaction never required a voice. It only ever required your intent, carried accurately.

Between them that is a far larger group than “accessibility” usually implies. And the truth is it doesn’t stop there: ask anyone who uses a drive-through weekly how often the bag is right. Everybody has been handed the wrong order and driven off anyway, because arguing through a speaker costs more than the sandwich. The difference is that for some people it isn’t an annoyance — it’s being shut out of a ninety-second transaction everyone else completes without thinking. Spling removes the requirement, not the difficulty.

08 — What only Spling does

Three things nobody else is building.

Ordering, payments, reservations and merchant integrations are becoming commodity infrastructure. We inherit them wherever they exist — they are not the advantage. These three are.

  • 01
    Moat

    Language composition

    You communicate in whatever language you naturally use. Spling does not merely translate what you said — translation is another lossy hop, and it still leaves a sentence for someone to interpret.

    It composes a validated structured transaction that matches the business’s live system: real items, real modifiers, real quantities, checked against what they actually offer right now. They receive exactly what their system understands. It cannot invent something, mishear a size, or pass along free text — and that constraint is what makes accuracy possible where speech fails.

  • 02
    Moat

    The communication profile

    Speech is optional. A speech difference, deafness, aphasia, autism, anxiety, a temporary injury, or simply being impossible to hear over bad audio — Spling carries that profile everywhere you go, along with your language, your allergens and what you actually like.

    You never have to explain yourself again. Not at a new counter, not in a new city, not to a new assistant. It holds the negatives too — what you sent back, what you never order twice — so “the usual” is a real instruction and a surprise stays inside your allergens. An anaphylaxis allergen never depends on you remembering to mention it: it is blocked before the transaction exists.

    And it learns how you are misheard. If your assistant hears “thauthage” every time you say sausage, that is not a fact about you — it is a fact about a speech recogniser, and Spling records it once so it never costs you again. The same machinery that folds lattét onto Latte works on a lisp, a stammer, an accent, a cold. It is yours alone, it is never pooled with anyone else’s, and it goes with you when you leave.

    It is also deliberately not allowed to guess. A correction only ever applies where your own words failed, it is kept only if the result is actually better, and it can never decide between two things that both fit — that stays a question. It will not touch no, without or allergic at all: a dropped negation is not a wrong order, it is the thing you cannot eat. Those get confirmed back to you in writing rather than guessed at.

  • 03
    Moat

    Accuracy intelligence

    The valuable asset was never the ordering. It is owning the whole chain:

    1. Intent
    2. Structured transaction
    3. What the business received
    4. What actually arrived
    5. The correction

    Today the payment processor sees the ticket, the business sees the complaint, and nobody joins them across merchants. Every completed interaction improves the system — and this is operational intelligence, not reviews. It answers questions no star rating can:

    • Which locations are consistently accurate?
    • Which modifiers fail most often?
    • Which translations create confusion?
    • Which merchants improve over time?
    • Does Spling objectively reduce errors?

    This dataset compounds forever, and nobody starting later can back-date it.

Your profile isn’t in the assistant. It’s yours.

This is the part that matters. Your language, how you communicate, your allergens, what “the usual” means, every order you’ve ever placed — none of it lives inside Claude or ChatGPT. It lives with you, and it arrives the moment you connect.

So when you switch assistants — to whatever is best next year, or whatever your school, workplace, or country puts in front of you — you don’t start over. You don’t re-explain your speech difference to a new machine. You don’t retype your allergens and hope. You connect, and it already knows.

And it’s exportable, in the open PAM format, any time you want it. Take it and leave. Nothing about Spling holds you here — that’s the whole point of a profile you own.

10 — Being built next

What comes after the connector

None of this is live yet. It’s written down here because it’s what the architecture is being built to allow, and we’d rather be held to a published list than quietly ship less.

  • The code on your wrist

    A pickup code is four characters and a tap — it belongs on a watch, not buried in a phone. Someone stages an order for you; it arrives on your wrist; you raise it at the counter and it’s verified. No app to find, no screen to unlock, no explaining yourself at the window. For a caregiver ordering remotely, that hand-off is the whole interaction.

  • Context that loads before you ask

    Arriving somewhere known should be enough. The business, what they offer right now, your language, your profile, your requirements — all present before you open anything. The location is only the trigger; anyone can draw a circle on a map. What loads inside it is the part worth building.

  • Beyond the menu

    The same structured composition applies to a hotel request, a pharmacy counter, an airline desk or a government service window. Wherever a business publishes what it offers, Spling can compose an exact request against it — ordering is just the first catalogue we pointed it at.

  • Menus you’ve programmed already

    Your regular orders, saved and named, ready to send in one action — the same idea as an AAC board, applied to ordering. If you eat the same thing at the same place every week, you should never have to compose it twice, and the person behind the counter should get an identical ticket every time.

  • Nothing that has to be heard

    For deaf and hard-of-hearing customers the failure isn’t only speaking — it’s the reply. “Was that a medium?” shouted through a speaker is the part that ends the transaction. Order status, substitutions, sold-out items and the ready signal all arrive as text you can read at your own pace, and anything the restaurant needs to ask comes back the same way.

  • Caregiver staging, properly

    A parent, support worker or partner composes; the person the order belongs to confirms. Their allergens, their profile, their confirmation — a caregiver can prepare an order but never silently place one. Independence is the point, so the last tap is always theirs.
